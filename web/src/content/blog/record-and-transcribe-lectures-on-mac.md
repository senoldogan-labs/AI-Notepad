A 90-minute lecture moves fast. You're writing down one point while the lecturer is already on the next, and the slide you needed is gone. Recording the lecture and getting a transcript afterwards means you can listen properly in the room and catch up on the details later.

A Mac can do this well, with free tools or with an app built for it. This guide covers what to ask before you record, how to get good audio from a lecture hall, the free options already on your Mac, and how to turn a transcript into notes you'll actually revise from.

## First: ask

Before anything technical, check whether you're allowed to record.

- **Your university's policy.** Many universities have rules about recording lectures, sometimes in the student handbook, sometimes per course. Some allow it for personal study; some require permission.
- **The lecturer.** Even where it's allowed, asking is polite and often gets you a better seat or a copy of the slides. "Would you mind if I record the lecture audio for my own notes?" is enough.
- **Disability and accessibility support.** If you need recordings because of a disability or learning difference, your university's accessibility service can usually arrange official permission.

Recordings for your own study are one thing. Sharing them (with classmates, online) is another, and usually needs explicit permission, because the lecture belongs to the lecturer and the university. Keep your recordings to yourself unless you've been told otherwise.

## Getting good audio in a lecture hall

A lecture is easier to record than a meeting in one way: there's mostly one speaker. It's harder in another: that speaker may be twenty metres away in a room with lots of echo.

- **Sit near the front.** Distance is the biggest enemy. The first few rows make a much bigger difference than any setting.
- **Check whether the lecture is amplified.** If the lecturer uses a microphone and speakers, sit near a speaker; the recording will pick up the amplified voice clearly.
- **Keep the laptop open, facing forward.** The MacBook's microphones sit near the keyboard or the top edge. Don't bury the laptop behind a bag.
- **Avoid typing noise if you can.** Heavy typing right next to the microphones can be louder than a distant lecturer. If you type a lot, an external microphone helps.
- **Consider a small external microphone** for big halls: a USB microphone pointed at the front, selected in **System Settings › Sound › Input**.

If the lecture is online, for example on Zoom or Teams, the situation is different: the lecturer's voice comes through your Mac's speakers, and an app that records system audio gets a clean copy. We explained how that works in [Recording Teams, Zoom and Google Meet on a Mac](/blog/record-teams-zoom-google-meet-on-mac).

## The free options on your Mac

**Apple Notes.** On recent versions of macOS, Notes can record audio and transcribe it, on Macs with Apple silicon. Open a note, start a recording, and the transcript appears alongside it. With Apple Intelligence turned on, you can also get a summary of the transcript. Apple notes that transcription isn't available in every language or region, so check that yours is supported.

**Voice Memos.** Fine for recording; you can transcribe the file afterwards with a separate app.

**Built-in Dictation.** Not for lectures. It's made for your own voice, typed into a text field, not for recording another person for 90 minutes. (It's great for dictating your own notes afterwards; see our guide to [voice typing on a Mac](/blog/voice-typing-on-mac).)

For many students, Notes is enough: free, simple, and the recording and transcript sit next to your notes.

## When an app is worth it

A dedicated transcription or note app can help when:

- **The lecture isn't in your first language.** A live transcript, and especially a live translation, helps you follow along in the room, not just afterwards. We wrote about [following meetings in a language you're still learning](/blog/follow-a-meeting-in-a-language-you-are-learning), and the same habits apply to lectures.
- **You want structured notes**, not just a transcript: key concepts, definitions, the things the lecturer said would be on the exam.
- **You want to search across the whole course.** "Where did she explain the difference between the two methods?" is easy across 12 lectures if they're all in one searchable place.
- **Your lectures are online** and you want system audio captured as well as your microphone.

## Turning a transcript into study notes

A 90-minute transcript can run to 10,000–15,000 words. Nobody revises from that. Turn it into something shorter on the same day, while you still remember the lecture:

1. **Pull out the structure.** What were the three to five main topics? Make them headings.
2. **Collect definitions.** Every "X is defined as…" goes into a glossary at the top. These are exam material.
3. **Mark the signals.** Lecturers flag important things: "this will come up again", "a common mistake is…", "make sure you understand…". Search the transcript for phrases like these.
4. **Write questions, not just answers.** For each topic, write one or two questions you should be able to answer. Revising becomes testing yourself, which works much better than rereading.
5. **Link to the slides.** Note the slide numbers next to each topic so you can find the visuals later.

If you use an AI tool to help, check its summary against the transcript for anything that will be examined. Summaries are good at the main points and sometimes wrong on the details, and the details are what you'll be marked on.

## The short version

- Ask first: university policy, the lecturer, and the accessibility service if relevant.
- Sit near the front or near a speaker; an external microphone helps in big halls.
- Apple Notes can record and transcribe on Apple silicon Macs, for free.
- Use an app for live translation, structured notes or searching across a course.
- Turn each transcript into topics, definitions and questions on the same day.
