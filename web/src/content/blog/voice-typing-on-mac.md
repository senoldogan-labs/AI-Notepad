Most people speak much faster than they type. That's why voice typing has quietly become one of the most popular uses of AI on the desktop: a new wave of dictation apps promises to turn rambling speech into clean text in any app, and some of them charge a monthly subscription for it.

But every Mac already has Dictation built in, for free. So when is it enough, and when is an app worth it? This guide covers both, plus the privacy question most people forget to ask.

## Built-in Dictation: how to turn it on

macOS Dictation works in any text field: Mail, Slack, Notes, the browser, a code editor.

1. Open **System Settings › Keyboard**.
2. Scroll to **Dictation** and switch it on.
3. Choose a **Shortcut**. The default on recent Macs is the microphone key (🎤) in the top row; you can also pick "Press Control key twice" or set your own.
4. Under **Languages**, click **Edit** to add the languages you dictate in.

To dictate, put the cursor where you want the text, press the shortcut and talk. Press it again (or Escape) to stop.

A few things make built-in Dictation better than people expect:

- **Punctuation is automatic** in many languages, and you can still say "comma", "full stop" or "new line".
- **On Apple silicon Macs, many languages are processed on the device**, so your speech doesn't leave the Mac. Apple's feature availability page lists which.
- **You can type and dictate at the same time.** The keyboard keeps working while Dictation is listening.

## Where built-in Dictation falls short

It's good, not perfect. The common complaints:

- **Raw speech in, raw speech out.** It writes what you said, including the "so, um, basically" at the start. It doesn't tidy up your sentences.
- **Jargon and names.** Product names, colleagues' names and technical terms often come out wrong.
- **Toggle, not hold.** Many people prefer push-to-talk: hold a key while speaking, let go when done. Built-in Dictation's shortcut toggles on and off.
- **Language switching.** If you speak more than one language in a day, you have to change the dictation language yourself.

If none of these bother you, stop here: built-in Dictation is free, private and already on your Mac.

## What dictation apps add

Dictation apps tend to fall into three groups.

**AI-cleanup apps.** These transcribe your speech, then use a language model to remove filler words, fix grammar and sometimes reformat (a list becomes bullet points; a rambling message becomes three clear sentences). Wispr Flow is the best known. As of November 2026, its pricing page lists a free plan with a limited number of words per week, and Pro at $15 a month, or $12 a month billed annually.

**Local-model apps.** Apps such as Superwhisper run speech recognition models on your Mac, with optional cloud models. They're popular with people who want more control over models and modes. Most have a free tier and a paid plan; check their sites for current prices.

**Voice typing inside another app you already use.** Some meeting note apps now include voice typing as a side feature. Granola lists voice typing in any app on its free plan (in beta on Mac and Windows, checked November 2026), and Gistsmith, the app we make, includes it free with any account.

For transcribing existing audio files (an interview, a podcast, a voice memo), a file transcription app such as MacWhisper is a better fit than a dictation app.

## The privacy question

Voice typing sees everything you dictate: emails to clients, messages to your partner, notes about patients or salaries. Before you pick an app, find out where your speech goes.

- **On the device or in the cloud?** On-device recognition means the audio never leaves your Mac. Cloud recognition can be more accurate for some languages, but your voice is sent to a server.
- **Is AI cleanup a separate step?** Some apps transcribe locally but send the text to a cloud language model to polish it. That's still your text leaving the Mac.
- **What's kept, and for how long?** Look for a stated retention period, and whether your dictations are used to train models.
- **Does it read your screen?** Some apps capture the surrounding context (the app you're in, nearby text) to improve accuracy. Useful, but know that it's happening.

None of these are deal-breakers on their own. You just want to know the answers before you dictate something confidential.

## Habits that make dictated text clean

Whatever you use, a few habits do more for quality than switching apps:

- **Think first, then speak a whole sentence.** Dictation handles complete sentences much better than fragments with long pauses.
- **Use a decent microphone.** AirPods or a headset beat a laptop microphone in a noisy room.
- **Dictate drafts, edit by keyboard.** Speaking is fast for getting words down; the keyboard is still faster for fixing a single word.
- **Say punctuation in long texts.** Even with automatic punctuation, saying "new paragraph" gives you structure.
- **Keep a list of the names that go wrong.** Many apps let you add custom words. Ten names cover most mistakes.

## Where voice typing works best

Voice typing shines for:

- **Replies to messages and emails**, especially longer ones.
- **First drafts** of documents, where speed matters more than polish.
- **Notes right after a meeting**, while you still remember what happened. If a meeting app already wrote the note, you can dictate your own additions underneath.
- **Prompts for AI tools.** People write longer, more specific prompts when they speak them, and longer prompts usually get better answers.

It's less useful in open-plan offices, for code, and for anything where exact formatting matters on the first try.
