You moved for the job, and the job is in English. Then the Monday meeting starts in Finnish, because five of the six people in the room are Finnish and it's simply faster for them. You catch every third word, nod at the right moments, and leave not quite sure whether something was just assigned to you.

This happens to a lot of people: expats in Helsinki, Berlin or Stockholm, people working with a client in another country, anyone whose team switched languages without asking. You're good at your job, and for an hour you feel like you aren't.

Here are the habits that help most, and how a Mac can help with the rest.

## Before the meeting

**Ask for the agenda.** A one-line agenda tells you what each part of the meeting is about. Knowing the topic does more for comprehension than knowing the words: once you know they're discussing the release date, "neljästoista" is much easier to place.

**Learn the meeting's vocabulary, not the language's.** Every team repeats the same twenty or thirty words: the product names, the customers, "deadline", "budget", "next week", the days of the week, numbers. Learn those first. They carry most of the meaning in a work meeting, and they're a lot smaller than a whole language.

**Tell one person.** Pick a colleague you trust and say, "I follow most of it, but if something lands on me, could you tell me afterwards in English?" Most people are happy to. Some will start switching to English for the important parts without being asked.

## During the meeting

**Listen for structure, not every word.** You don't need to understand each sentence. Listen for the signals that mark decisions and tasks: names, dates, numbers, and question intonation. In Finnish, "sovitaanko" ("shall we agree") and "hoidatko" ("will you take care of") are worth learning on day one.

**Write down what you hear, even if you're unsure.** A half-understood note like "Mikko — pricing — Thursday?" is easy to confirm later. Nothing at all is not.

**Ask once, and ask specifically.** "Sorry, could you say that in English?" every five minutes gets tiring for everyone. One precise question near the end works better: "Just to check: am I doing the release notes by Friday?" It shows you followed most of it, and it only takes ten seconds.

**Speak in the language you're comfortable in.** In many international teams in the Nordics, answering in English in a Finnish meeting is perfectly normal. Saying something clearly is worth more than saying it in the room's language.

## After the meeting

**Confirm what's yours, in writing.** Send a short message within the hour: "Thanks. From my side: release notes by Friday, pricing table to Ayşe today. Anything I missed?" It catches misunderstandings while they're still cheap. We wrote more about [turning a meeting into a task list](/blog/turn-a-meeting-into-a-task-list).

**Keep a list of new words.** Write down the two or three terms you didn't know and look them up. After a month you'll be surprised how much of the meeting has become familiar.

## Where live translation helps

Habits get you a long way, but in a fast discussion you're still guessing. This is the gap live translation fills: the meeting runs in its own language, and you read a translation of each sentence as it's said.

It doesn't replace learning the language. It changes what you spend your attention on. Instead of decoding every sentence, you follow the discussion, notice when your name comes up, and pick up words by seeing them next to their meaning, again and again.

A few things are worth knowing before you rely on it:

- **It's a little behind.** Each sentence is translated after it's spoken, so there's a delay of a few seconds. Fine for following along, less so for jumping into a fast exchange.
- **Names and jargon are the weak spot.** A product name or a customer's name can come out wrong. Tools that let you add a list of names and terms do noticeably better.
- **Tell people you're using it.** If a tool is listening to the meeting, the people in it should know. One sentence at the start is enough; here's [how to say it without making it awkward](/blog/meeting-notes-on-mac-without-a-bot).

## The short version

- Learn the team's twenty words before the language's twenty thousand.
- Listen for names, dates and numbers; that's where the tasks are.
- Ask one precise question instead of many vague ones.
- Confirm your tasks in writing within the hour.
- Use live translation to follow the discussion, and let the glossary teach you the words.

The meeting will still be in Finnish next week. With the right habits, and a translation running underneath while you learn, it stops being an hour you just sit through.
