Most people don't need convincing that they have too many meetings. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index described an "infinite workday": people interrupted by a meeting, email or notification every two minutes on average during core hours, meetings after 8 pm up 16% year on year, and nearly half of employees saying their work feels chaotic and fragmented. In an Atlassian survey of 5,000 knowledge workers published in 2024, 78% said they struggle to get their work done because of how many meetings they're expected to attend.

The usual advice is "make it async". That's right for some meetings and wrong for others, and the hard part is telling which is which. This checklist helps you decide, meeting by meeting, and then shows how to run the async version so people actually read it.

## The four questions

For each recurring meeting on your calendar, answer these honestly.

### 1. Is information flowing one way?

If one person talks and everyone else listens, the meeting is a broadcast. Status updates, announcements and "here's what changed" sessions are the classic examples.

**One way → good candidate for async.** A written update or a short video does the job, and people read it at a time that suits them.

### 2. Does it need a decision with real trade-offs?

Some decisions are easy: one option is clearly better, and someone just needs to say yes. Others involve trade-offs, disagreement and people changing their minds as they hear each other.

**Easy decision → async.** Write the proposal, ask for objections by a deadline, decide.
**Contested decision → keep it live**, but send the written proposal beforehand so the meeting is spent deciding, not explaining.

### 3. Does the relationship or the mood matter?

One-on-ones, difficult feedback, conflict, onboarding a new colleague, the first meeting with a client: these are about people as much as information. Tone of voice and the chance to react in the moment matter.

**Relationship matters → keep it live.** These are often the meetings people value most.

### 4. Is it brainstorming or genuine problem-solving?

Fast back-and-forth, building on each other's ideas, sketching on a whiteboard: written threads are slow at this.

**Live problem-solving → keep it live**, but consider collecting ideas in writing first so quieter people get a fair say.

## Sort your calendar into three buckets

Put your recurring meetings into three buckets:

- **Replace with async:** weekly status updates, project check-ins where nothing is blocked, announcements, demos that could be a recording, "FYI" meetings.
- **Shorten:** stand-ups (move updates to writing, keep the call for blockers only), planning meetings (send the proposal first), all-hands (pre-record the updates, keep the Q&A live).
- **Keep:** one-on-ones, retrospectives, contested decisions, client relationship meetings, workshops, anything sensitive.

Try it on your own calendar. Most people find at least one meeting in the first bucket, and several in the second.

## Making async actually work

Async fails when it means "a long message nobody reads". It works when the format is short, structured and has a clear ask.

### The written update

Use the same structure every time:

```
Project X, week 48

Done
- Beta release notes published.
- Pricing page updated.

Next
- Ayşe: support training, by Wednesday.
- Me: onboarding emails, by Friday.

Blocked / need input
- Need a decision on the trial length by Thursday. Options below.
```

People learn where to look. The "Blocked / need input" section is the only one most readers need, so put names on it.

### The decision request

For decisions that don't need a meeting:

1. One paragraph of context.
2. The options, each in one or two lines.
3. Your recommendation.
4. **A deadline:** "If there are no objections by Thursday 12:00, we'll go with option B."

The deadline is what makes it work. Without it, async decisions drift for weeks.

### The recorded walkthrough

For demos and explanations, a short screen recording beats a meeting: people can watch at 1.5× speed, rewind, and skip the parts they know. Keep it under five minutes, and put the key decisions or questions in writing alongside it, because nobody searches a video.

## When you keep the meeting, keep it short

For the meetings that stay live:

- **Every invite has a goal.** "Decide the trial length" is a goal; "Pricing sync" isn't. If you can't write the goal in one line, the meeting probably isn't ready.
- **Pre-reads go out a day before**, and the meeting starts from the assumption that people read them.
- **End with tasks**: who does what, by when. Our [task list method](/blog/turn-a-meeting-into-a-task-list) explains how.
- **Send a follow-up within the hour.** The [follow-up email template](/blog/meeting-follow-up-email-template) takes five minutes.

## Notes are the bridge between live and async

Here's a less obvious point: good meeting notes are what make it possible for people to *skip* meetings.

If the notes from a meeting are reliable (decisions, tasks with owners and dates, a short summary), people who don't need to be there can read them afterwards in two minutes instead of attending for thirty. That's especially true across time zones; Microsoft's report found that nearly a third of meetings now span several.

Without notes, everyone attends "just in case". With notes, attendance can be optional for anyone who only needs to know the outcome.

## The short version

- One-way information, easy decisions → async.
- Relationships, contested decisions, live problem-solving → keep live.
- Async needs structure: a fixed update format, decision requests with deadlines, short recordings.
- Live meetings need a goal in the invite, a pre-read, and tasks at the end.
- Reliable notes let people skip meetings without missing outcomes.
