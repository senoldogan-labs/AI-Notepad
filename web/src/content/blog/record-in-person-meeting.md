Most advice about meeting notes assumes a video call. But plenty of important meetings still happen around a table: the weekly team meeting in the office, a workshop with a client, a board meeting, a parent–teacher evening, a site visit.

In-person meetings are harder to record well. There's no clean audio feed from each person, voices come from different distances, and someone always taps the table right next to the microphone. The good news is that a laptop and a bit of preparation get you most of the way to a transcript that's actually useful.

## What you need

For most meetings of up to six or eight people:

- **A laptop** with a recording or note app. A MacBook's built-in microphones are good enough for a small room.
- **A quiet-ish room.** Not silent, just not a café at lunchtime.
- **Two minutes before the meeting** to place things and do a test.

For larger rooms or long tables, add an external microphone (more on that below).

## Where to put the laptop

Placement matters more than anything else you can control.

**In the middle of the table, not at your seat.** If the laptop sits in front of you, your voice is loud and clear and the person at the far end is a murmur. Put it where the distance to everyone is roughly the same.

**Lid open, microphones facing the room.** On MacBooks the microphones sit near the keyboard or along the top edge. Don't close the lid or tuck the laptop behind a water jug.

**Away from noise.** Keep it away from the projector fan, the air vent and the coffee machine. Ask people not to put cups and phones right next to it; a mug set down on the table can be the loudest sound in the recording.

**One device, not five.** If several people record on their own laptops, you'll have five half-good recordings instead of one good one. Agree who records.

## When an external microphone helps

The built-in microphones work well when people sit fairly close. Farther away, voices get quieter and the room's echo gets louder. Consider an external microphone if:

- The table seats more than eight people.
- The room is large or echoey, like a glass meeting room or a hall.
- People are spread out, for example in a workshop where they stand and move.

A **USB conference microphone** (the flat, round kind that sits in the middle of the table) is made for this. A small **USB or wireless lavalier** works well for a single main speaker, like a lecturer or a presenter. Select it as the input device in **System Settings › Sound › Input** before you start.

## Do a 20-second test

Before people start talking for real, record a few seconds while someone at the far end of the table says a sentence. Check that it comes through clearly in the transcript. It's much better to find out now that the far end is inaudible than to find out afterwards.

## Ask the room

Recording an in-person meeting has the same consent rules as a call, and the social side is more direct: everyone can see the laptop.

Say it at the start, before the agenda:

> I'd like to record this so I can write proper notes. The recording stays with me, and I'll share the notes afterwards. Is everyone OK with that?

Wait for an answer. If someone isn't comfortable, don't record; take notes by hand. If the conversation turns to something personal or confidential, offer to pause.

Rules about recording conversations vary between countries, and in some places everyone present must agree. If you meet people from other organizations, or the meeting is formal, like a board meeting, check your organization's policy too. ## Helping the transcript tell people apart

Speaker labels are harder in a room than on a call, because everyone comes through the same microphone. A few habits help both the software and anyone reading the notes later:

- **Do a round of names at the start.** "Ayşe, product. Mikko, sales." It costs ten seconds.
- **Use names when you hand over.** "Mikko, what do you think?" makes the transcript much easier to follow.
- **One conversation at a time.** Side conversations are the main reason in-person transcripts become hard to read.
- **Say decisions and tasks out loud.** "So we're agreed: we launch on the 14th. Ayşe, you'll send the pricing by Thursday." A clear sentence like that turns into a clear line in the notes.

## After the meeting

A recording on its own isn't the goal. Within an hour of the meeting:

1. **Check the tasks.** Make sure each one has an owner and a date. Our [task list method](/blog/turn-a-meeting-into-a-task-list) explains how.
2. **Send a short follow-up** with decisions and next steps to the people who were there. The [follow-up email template](/blog/meeting-follow-up-email-template) takes five minutes.
3. **Decide what to keep.** Often the notes are enough and the audio can go.

## Phones, recorders and laptops

You've probably seen dedicated AI voice recorders and phone apps for in-person meetings. They're handy when you don't have a laptop with you, such as on a site visit or in a corridor conversation.

For meetings around a table with a laptop already there, the laptop is usually the better recorder: bigger microphones, a full-size screen to watch the transcript, and the notes are already where you'll edit and send them.

Whatever you use, the rules above stay the same: put it in the middle, test it, ask the room.

## The short version

- Put the laptop in the middle of the table, lid open, away from cups and fans.
- Use an external microphone for big or echoey rooms; select it in Sound settings.
- Test with the person farthest away before you start.
- Ask the room for consent, out loud, every time.
- Names at the start, names on hand-over, one conversation at a time.
- Send the tasks and decisions within the hour.
